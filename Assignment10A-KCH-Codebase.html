Introduction and Overview

In this assignment, sentiment analysis is performed in two parts.

First, the workflow from Chapter 2 of Text Mining with R is replicated (Silge and Robinson 2017). Using Jane Austen’s published novels, the tidy text approach is reproduced to accomplish sentiment extraction, lexicon comparison, word frequency contributions, and narrative trajectories. The original methodology and examples are referenced from the online text at tidytextmining.com/sentiment.

Second, the analysis is extended to a completely different corpus and domain: contemporary sports journalism covering the Women’s National Basketball Association (WNBA). Using the tidyRSS package (McDonnell 2021) and rvest, recent ESPN WNBA news coverage is harvested, and the full text of each reporting piece is scraped. Matthew Jockers’ sentiment engine is applied via the syuzhet package (Jockers 2020), evaluating both raw and length-normalized sentiment alongside traditional dictionary methods.

Approach

  1. Replicate example workflow from Chapter 2 of Text Mining with R
  • Recreate the tidy sentiment mining workflow from Chapter 2 across Jane Austen’s novels, demonstrating word-level and chapter-level sentiment analysis.
  1. Ingest and scrape ESPN’s WNBA corpus
  • Ingest live headlines from the ESPN WNBA RSS feed and programmatically scrape full article bodies using rvest.
  1. Incorporate an additional lexicon
  • Apply the syuzhet lexicon to score full articles and sentence-level narrative trajectories, comparing its behavior against the bing dictionary.
  1. Evaluate domain Differences
  • Contrast the emotional dynamics of Jane Austen’s novels with modern sports reporting, identifying domain-specific lexicon limitations and normalizing for document length.

Step 2: Extension to ESPN’s WNBA News

While RSS feeds provide immediate, structured access to media headlines, RSS descriptions are typically restricted to short teaser blurbs. To create a rich corpus suitable for narrative-level analysis, the article hyperlinks embedded in the feed are used to extract the complete paragraph text (<p>) from ESPN’s website.

Data loading

Real-time media analysis begins with data collection. The tidyRSS package (McDonnell 2021) is used to query ESPN’s live WNBA RSS endpoint. While RSS feeds provide structured access to new stories, the feed itself contains only article headlines and brief teaser descriptions.

Code 
wnba_feed <- tidyfeed("https://www.espn.com/espn/rss/wnba/news")

wnba_corpus <- wnba_feed |>
  select(title = item_title, text = item_description) |>
  mutate(full_text = paste(title, text))

To achieve an analytical depth comparable to the full-length chapters analyzed in Jane Austen’s novels, we cannot rely solely on 30-word summary blurbs. Using the canonical URLs captured in the item_link column, we build a custom web scraper using rvest to extract all paragraph elements from the body of each reporting piece. A tryCatch wrapper is included to handle potential network timeouts or paywall redirects gracefully, ensuring the Quarto document compiles cleanly without breaking if an individual link fails.

Code 
if (file.exists("wnba_data.rds")) {
  wnba_full_articles <- readRDS("wnba_data.rds")
} else {
  # your existing scraping code...

get_article_text <- function(url) {
  tryCatch({
    read_html(url) |>
      html_elements("p") |>
      html_text2() |>
      paste(collapse = " ")
  }, error = function(e) NA_character_)
}

wnba_full_articles <- wnba_feed |>
  select(title = item_title, 
         link = item_link) |>
  mutate(full_article_text = map_chr(link, 
                                     get_article_text)) |>
  filter(!is.na(full_article_text))

saveRDS(wnba_full_articles, "wnba_data.rds")
}

Analysis and Visualizations

To evaluate the sports corpus, three distinct analyses are executed:

1. Measuring overall polarity using Matthew Jockers’ syuzhet dictionary.

2. Extracting the most frequent positive and negative sentiment drivers via bing.

3. Scaling sentiment by word count to remove length bias, and plotting the sentence-by-sentence narrative arc of the longest feature story.

First, evaluating the initial feed text using the syuzhet lexicon (Jockers 2020) is undertaken. Unlike binary dictionaries that simply tally words, the Syuzhet engine assigns continuous emotional valence to sentences and short texts.

The word contribution plot ( Figure 3 ) illustrates both the strengths and the inherent limitations of using general-purpose lexicons on specialized sports data. First, terms like “lead”, “award”, “championship”, and “top” may dominate the positive facet, reflecting celebratory coverage of seasonal awards and championship milestones. Second, terms like “loss”, “foul”, “cold”, and “offensive” may populate the negative facet. In sports analytics, these are neutral, mechanical descriptors of play rather than indicators of genuine negative sentiment. This parallels Silge & Robinson’s finding regarding the word “miss” in Austen’s novels.

Most notably, team names introduce severe sentiment distortion: ‘fever’ (Indiana Fever) appears as the single largest negative word, while ‘golden’ (Golden State Valkyries) and ‘liberty’ (New York Liberty) drive top positive counts. Furthermore, ‘defensive’ is penalized as negative while ‘defeated’ scores positive.

When analyzing long-form journalism, raw cumulative sentiment is inherently confounded by article length: a 2,500-word feature story will naturally amass a higher total score than a 250-word roster bulletin simply due to word volume. To resolve this length bias, we compute a length-normalized sentiment index:

\(Normalized Score=\frac{Syuzhet Cumulative Sentiment}{Total Word Count }×100\)

This represents the net emotional valence per 100 words of prose.

Code 
wnba_scored <- wnba_full_articles |>
  mutate(
    article_id = row_number(),
    clean_title = stringr::str_replace_all(title, "[^\x01-\x7F]+", ""),
    short_title = stringr::str_trunc(clean_title, 35),
     word_count = stringr::str_count(full_article_text, "\\w+"),
    sentiment_score = get_sentiment(full_article_text, method = "syuzhet"),
    sentiment_normalized = (sentiment_score / word_count) * 100
  )

wnba_scored |> 
  ggplot(aes(x = reorder(short_title, sentiment_normalized), 
             y = sentiment_normalized, 
             fill = sentiment_score > 0)) +
  geom_col(show.legend = FALSE) +
  coord_flip() +
  scale_fill_manual(values = c("TRUE" = "#2ca02c", "FALSE" = "#d62728")) +
  theme_minimal() +
  labs(
    title = "WNBA News Displays Postseason Bliss (Syuzhet Lexicon)",
    x = NULL,
    y = "Net Sentiment Score (Green = Positive, Red = Negative)"
  ) +
  theme_standard()

Normalizing by length reveals that the highest sentiment concentrations belong to forward-looking, celebratory headlines (such as championship parade reporting and franchise dynasty declarations). Even after adjusting for document length, every single article remains net positive, highlighting the pervasive promotional optimism of the WNBA offseason media cycle.

In Chapter 2, Silge and Robinson demonstrated how Austen’s novels can be viewed as narrative arcs with emotional peaks and valleys. To determine whether modern sports journalism exhibits similar narrative arcs, the longest article in the corpus is isolated, segmented it into individual sentences, and sentence-level sentiment progression is tracked from start to finish. A LOESS smoothing curve is overlaid to capture the underlying emotional trajectory.

The trajectory plot illustrates the structural progression of an in-depth sports postseason outlook:

  • The curve begins with elevated positive sentiment (\(\approx +0.8\)), likely opening with celebratory reflections on Golden State’s championship victory.
  • Between sentences 100 and 220, the trajectory descends to a lower plateau (\(\approx +0.25\)). Here, the reporting likely shifts toward runner-up Atlanta’s heartbreak, pending free-agency decisions, and roster turnover.
  • Unlike a historical ranking, this forward-looking piece rebounds upward near sentence 300 (\(\approx +0.5\)). The article concludes on an upbeat note, likely focusing on draft assets, recovery timelines, and title aspirations for the upcoming season.

Findings and Recommendations

In this work, a sentiment analysis workflow was replicated from Chapter 2 of Text Mining with R (Silge and Robinson 2017). The workflow was then modified for analysis of modern sports media, particularly coverage of the WNBA by ESPN (@espn_wnba). Comparing Regency literature to contemporary sports writing reveals significant differences in corpus tone, lexicon accuracy, and structural length.

In Jane Austen’s novels, sentiment trajectories fluctuate dramatically above and below zero, capturing the emotional rhythm of the novels. In contrast, the ESPN WNBA corpus is overwhelmingly right-skewed and positive. Every single article evaluated via the `syuzhet` lexicon produced a net positive score.

This reflects the editorial cycle of modern sports journalism: following the conclusion of the WNBA Finals, reporting naturally pivots away from high-stakes game conflict writing to celebratory features, expansion team anticipation, mock drafts, and historical championship rankings.

A central takeaway from Chapter 2 of Text Mining with R is that generic sentiment dictionaries struggle when words have domain-specific connotations (e.g., the word “miss” in Austen’s works) (Silge and Robinson 2017). This issue is magnified in sports analytics when using the bing lexicon. Words such as “defensive”, “lost”, and “injury” are penalized as negative, even though in sports reporting these are standard descriptors of tactical play. Most strikingly, franchise names introduce substantial distortion: “fever” (Indiana Fever) registered as the single largest negative word in the corpus, while “golden” (Golden State Valkyries) and “liberty” (New York Liberty) dominated positive sentiment counts.

In the raw Syuzhet scoring, article sentiment scores ranged from \(+1\) to over \(+35\). However, this variance was largely driven by document length rather than emotional intensity: longer investigative pieces accumulated dozens of mild positive words simply by volume.

By implementing a length-normalized sentiment metric (\(\frac{\text{Sentiment Score}}{\text{Word Count}} \times 100\)), a more apt comparison could be made. This demonstrated that shorter, punchy headlines often possessed higher per-word emotional density than multi-page retrospectives.

This work could be extended in two key ways. First, future sports text mining should establish a domain-specific exclusion list that neutralizes terms like “steal”, “foul”, and “turnover”. Second, rather than scoring entire articles, combining sentiment scoring with Named Entity Recognition (NER) would allow researchers to separate fan sentiment toward specific players (e.g., Caitlin Clark or Breanna Stewart) from general game reporting.
